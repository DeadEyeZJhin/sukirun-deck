A distributor in Bacolod City. Field agents visit sari-sari stores and take orders for noodles, sauces and sweets.

In the shop

In the group chat

At the warehouse

At the office

The rule I build by: anything that makes a person redo work by hand is worth removing. Every screen in this deck exists because it removes one of those steps.

Accounts

Everyone logs in, and sees only their job

One app, five kinds of screen. The office decides who is what.

Accounts

  • Email and password, per person
  • A new sign-up starts with nothing — the office gives the role
  • Switch someone off and their phone stops getting the list

Five roles

  • Field agent — takes orders, sees only their own shops
  • Admin / secretary — confirms, sees everyone
  • Warehouse — products, prices, pictures
  • Delivery — packs and delivers
  • Store owner — orders directly
The rules are enforced by the database, not just by hiding buttons. An agent's phone is never sent another agent's customers.

"We don't want to leave our spreadsheet"

Keep it. The app can read it and write to it.

An app can work with a Google Sheet instead of replacing it. The one rule: every list has one place where it is typed.

Sheet → app

Your sheet stays the price list. The office edits products and prices in the sheet, as today. Every phone picks them up at its next sync.

Safe

App → sheet

Orders land in your sheet. Every order becomes a new row, with a daily-totals tab. Your filters, formulas and pivot tables keep working.

Safe

Both ways, same list

Possible, but I'd advise against it. Two people change the same number in two places and the last one silently wins. A sheet has no "who is allowed", no history, and cannot be edited offline.

Risky
Month 1

Nothing changes

The sheet stays. The app reads it and writes orders into it.

→
Month 2

One list moves

Whichever hurts most — usually orders — is typed in the app only.

→
When they're ready

The rest follow

Or never. The sheet can stay a report for as long as they like it.

Learned the hard way on SukiRun: prices started in a Google Sheet and moved into the app, because two places owning the same price quietly disagreed. A spreadsheet is a great report and a risky boss.
SpeakerThis answers the objection before it is raised: nobody has to throw anything away on day one. The app joins their sheet, then earns its place one list at a time.
How I work

I measure before I build

The app records what really happens, so I decide from that — not from guessing.

1
Follow the work

Find every place something is written down twice.

2
Build for both ends

The phone in the field and the PC in the office.

3
Test in the real app

On a phone and on a PC, with made-up data — never a real customer's.

4
Ship one version

With what changed written in plain words for the people using it.

What I chose not to build is part of the work. A map of shops, reports and payment tracking were all designed — and parked, because the data showed nobody would use them yet. A screen nobody opens is cost, not value.
