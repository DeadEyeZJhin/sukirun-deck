Before

The order was typed out three times

A distributor in Bacolod City. Field agents visit sari-sari stores and take orders for noodles and confectionery.

In the shop

Items written in a notebook, the total worked out on a phone calculator. If the shopkeeper changed their mind, the whole column was added up again.

In the group chat

The same order typed into Messenger, numbered by hand — 1. …, 2. … — while standing in the street.

At the warehouse

Read off Messenger and retyped again to be picked and packed. Three chances for a number to change on the way.

The lesson that generalises: anything that makes somebody redo work by hand is worth killing. That is the whole brief.
SpeakerDo not oversell this. The app did not replace a system — there was no system. It replaced a notebook and a calculator.
Shape of the thing

One HTML file, two runtimes, five roles

One file, two runtimes

  • Markup, styles, logic and product photographs inlined into a single HTML document by a Python build script
  • No framework, no bundler, no runtime dependency
  • Android — that file in a WebView wrapper, signed, 4.7 MB
  • Browser — the office opens the same build on a desktop

Five roles, one account

  • Field Agent — visits shops, takes orders
  • Store owner — orders directly
  • Admin / Secretary — reviews, calls, confirms
  • Warehouse — products, prices, pictures
  • Delivery — packs and delivers

Step one

Pick the products

Two hundred products, a photograph on every card, and a running total that never leaves the bottom of the screen. The phone is often in the shopkeeper's hands, so the grid has to be readable by someone who has never seen the app.

  • This shop puts what they bought last time at the top — then Best sellers, Grouped, Default, and a search box.
  • Type a quantity or tap − / +. A redraw never steals the keyboard mid-entry.
  • The form is blank every time. Nothing carries over from the last shop, because a shopkeeper must never see another shop's order.
  • A price the office changes while the form is open updates the card and says so at the top. Typed quantities survive.

Step two

Check the basket

The running-total bar opens the basket. Every line is editable without leaving it, and each line carries its own unit.

  • Sold as — the same product by piece, pack, box or case. The unit and the price are frozen onto the line, so a price list rewritten next month cannot rewrite what a shopkeeper agreed to today.
  • Quantities change in place; only the numbers refresh, so the list never jumps under a thumb.
  • Three ways out: Send order to office, Keep adding, or Copy order summary — with the paste number beside it.

Step three

Send it, or copy it, or both

The summary, then the shop's details, then the way out. The order comes first and the paperwork comes after — the form used to open on five empty boxes, with the thing you are actually doing underneath them.

  • Send order to office writes it locally first and queues it. A dead zone never costs an order.
  • Send with a detail missing and the form glides down and puts that box in the middle of the screen, instead of jumping.
  • Nothing is silent. Walk away half-done and an amber card follows you onto every screen, naming the shop, until you send it, clear it, or throw it away.

The feature that gets used most

The paste is the product

What actually leaves the app is a Messenger message. Some agents never press Send at all — they add the order up, copy it, paste it in the group chat, and that was the visit. So the copied text is a designed artefact, not a debug dump.

Three decisions in that block

  • No map link. A maps.google.com URL unfurls in Messenger into a preview card that buries the order it was attached to. Removed after watching it happen.
  • The leading number. The group chat numbers orders by hand; on a phone that means scrolling the message box back to the top after pasting. The app types it, with a ‹ 3 › stepper beside the button.
  • One generator. Every copy, share and report path runs through the same function, so two screens can never drift apart.
SpeakerThe number is stored on the phone only — never synced, never sent to the office. It is where this phone got to today, not a fact about the order.
The hard part

Offline-first, not offline-tolerant

Every write lands on the phone first and is queued. The network is an optimisation, never a precondition.

1
Write hits local storage — the order exists
2
Queued as dirty, with a badge saying what is waiting
3
Signal returns → push over Supabase RPCs
4
Pull merges what other phones did meanwhile

What is stored where

  • localStorage — orders, shops, prices, settings
  • IndexedDB — photographs and map tiles, because they would blow the 5 MB quota in a week
  • Supabase Storage — shop pictures that need to reach other phones

What works with no signal

  • The whole catalogue, with photographs
  • Taking, editing and copying an order
  • The map, from cached tiles
  • Nearest shops, from GPS alone

The honest counter

"Waiting to send" counts what is genuinely unsent from this phone — not the queue length. A shop pulled down from the office was being re-queued to push straight back, and the badge read "40 waiting" on a phone with nothing of its own to send.

Two phones, one row

Per-row merge, not last-write-wins

Four people, four phones, everyone offline at different times. Last-write-wins silently destroys a colleague's work, and nobody finds out until a delivery goes to the wrong address.

Field-level rules

A merge decides per field, not per row. The office wins on names, pins and shop details; the phone wins on what it typed. Payments are merged server-side, so two phones recording against one order cannot lose one.

Tombstones

A deleted order, shop or product leaves a marker so the deletion actually propagates. A tombstone for something a phone has never seen is still remembered — otherwise the row arrives later and comes back from the dead.

A versioned repair

The merge rules carry a REPAIR_ID. Change them in a way that could have dropped data and the id is bumped, so a phone that already lost rows re-asks the office once — instead of never asking again.

Why it is written down: these people know each other socially. Deleting a friend's real work is worse than a bug.

Access

Enforced in the database, not the screen

Every staff phone holds a copy of the company's orders and shops. Hiding a card is not security, so the rules live in Postgres.

How

  • Row-level security on all eight tables
  • Every write goes through a security definer function — 29 of them; no table is writable directly
  • An optional 4-digit PIN on the Admin door
  • An on/off switch per person, so a lost phone stops reaching the shared list

Who sees what

  • Agent — their own orders and their own book of shops
  • Store owner — their own orders, nothing else
  • Warehouse — prices, pictures, groups. No Admin.
  • Admin — everything, including roles

The price is frozen onto the line

The unit and the price travel with the order line, not with the product. The office can rewrite the price list next month and it cannot rewrite what a shopkeeper agreed to today. That is one line of thought and about forty of code, and it is the difference between a catalogue and a record.

Keeping it true

  • Prices published from the office and pulled by every phone
  • Groups and folders, hidden products, delete and bring back
  • A hidden or deleted product already on an unsent order stays, and is marked
  • Removals travel to other phones as tombstones
The round

Who has gone quiet

Every shop carries its owner, contact, address, landmark, GPS pin, photograph, order history and running balance. Added on one phone, visible on all of them.

  • Due for a visit — a shop nobody has ordered from, or marked visited, in N days. The threshold is the agent's to set.
  • A blue strip for the agent, a count for the office. The reminder belongs to whoever does the walking.
  • I already visited closes the loop without needing an order — a visit that sold nothing is still a visit.
  • Sort A–Z or by Nearest, which works from GPS alone with no signal at all.

Offline

A map that still draws with no signal

MapLibre GL over OpenFreeMap tiles, with the tiles cached into IndexedDB as they are fetched. Once an area has been seen, it keeps working in the dead zones.

  • Pin a shop from the Stores tab or while taking the order; correct the pin later.
  • Overdue shops carry a different pin, so the round can be planned by looking.
  • No map link ever goes into the copied order — in Messenger it unfurls into a card that buries the order.
  • Nearest-shops is computed from raw GPS, so it answers even when nothing will load.

The other end, on a desktop

The office, the warehouse and the van

The same build, opened in a browser instead of wrapped in an APK. The office IS the warehouse here — Mark as Packed and Mark as Delivered sit on this screen as well as on the delivery phone, through one shared code path.

The list

Search, date window, sort and whose-orders — applied before the four figures at the top, so they always describe what is on screen. Status ladder Sent → Confirmed → Packed → Delivered.

Undo is not cancel

Taking a status back reads the step to return to out of the recorded stamps — not a fixed ladder — and writes itself into the timeline. Nothing rolls back silently.

What changed, not who

Editing an order records what changed. "Somebody edited this" is the least useful half of the answer when the office is about to ring the number on it.

From source to a phone in a shop

Shipping it

1
build.py inlines styles, logic and photographs into one HTML file
2
build_apk.py compiles, dexes, aligns and signs the APK
3
release.py writes update.json with the version, size and SHA-256
4
Published to GitHub Releases — the address every phone checks

How a phone updates itself

The app reads update.json from /releases/latest/download/, compares the version code against its own and offers the download. That URL is baked into the APK once and never changes, because GitHub keeps latest pointing at the newest release forever.

The rule that had to be learned

update.json carries one release's notes. A phone that skipped the version before this one is told nothing about it — so when the previous release may not be installed yet, its notes are merged in before uploading. Missed three times before it was written down.

The slide I am proudest of

What I chose not to build

Every one of these was asked for, or was obviously next. Each was measured against the real data first, and each would have drawn an empty screen.

Killed by the numbers

  • The delivery map
  • Money owed, as a screen of its own
  • Anything after "packed"
  • Reports for the boss
  • Undo sending an order
  • Discounts and free items

Parked with a reason

Push notifications. They need Firebase Cloud Messaging plus something server-side to send them. The wrapper has no Firebase and the app has no realtime — measured, not guessed. A phase of its own.

What ships instead: the app checks on open and puts a count on the tab. "3 new."

Why this matters

Four people use this app. A feature that only pays off once everybody is on it does not pay off. Each parked idea is recorded with the number that parked it — not with a shrug — so the argument does not have to be had twice.
